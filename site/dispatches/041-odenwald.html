Statecraft. Thursday, 6 November 1941 · Mid Atlantic · near the equator

The ship that lied

She flew the American flag and had "Willmoto, Philadelphia" painted on her stern. She was German, and her holds were full of rubber.

6 November 1941. A month and a day before Pearl Harbor, the cruiser USS Omaha and the destroyer USS Somers were on Neutrality Patrol when they sighted a freighter at dawn near the equator. She was really the German blockade runner Odenwald, out of Yokohama with about 3,857 metric tons of raw rubber.

She steered to dodge them and gave poor answers to their signals, so Captain Theodore E. Chandler sent over an armed boarding party. As the sailors climbed aboard, two explosions went off in the stern. The Germans had set scuttling charges and taken to the lifeboats.

The Americans ordered the crew back aboard, plugged the flooding and got the engines running. By evening she was under way.

The United States was not at war, and the legal ground was shaky. In 1947 a court awarded each man in the boarding party $3,000, often called the last prize money the U.S. Navy ever paid.

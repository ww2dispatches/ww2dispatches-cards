Campaign Series: Pearl Harbor · The Chronicle · Part 2 of 3

Climb Mount Niitaka

In four days Tokyo turns doubt into decision, and the carriers are told the date.

30 November to 3 December 1941

By the last day of November 1941 the carriers were four days out from the Kurils, steaming east through cold grey water with their radios silent. They could still be called home. Over the next four days, in a palace in Tokyo and an office in Pearl Harbor, that chance slipped away, and almost no one on the American side understood what they were seeing.

This is the second of three parts on the road to Pearl Harbor. Part 1 followed the fleet out of Hitokappu Bay and the war warning that pointed everywhere but Hawaii. Here the last doubts in Tokyo are heard and set aside, the order goes out, and Japan's diplomats begin to burn their secrets.

A Brother's Doubts

Imperial Palace, Tokyo, 30 November 1941

Day by Day · Day 5 of 13
6:30 a.m. MT, 30 Nov 2026

A prince warns his brother

The Emperor's younger brother brings him a message the admirals have not said out loud: the navy would rather not fight America.

Prince Takamatsu, a commander on the Naval General Staff, tells Emperor Hirohito that the navy hopes to avoid war. That afternoon the Emperor summons Prime Minister Tojo, then calls in Navy Minister Shigetaro Shimada and Admiral Osami Nagano, Chief of the Naval General Staff.

For twenty five minutes the two admirals answer him. They assure him the navy is prepared. The Imperial Conference will meet tomorrow.

Open the day page

Prince Takamatsu was the Emperor's younger brother and a serving naval officer, a commander on the Naval General Staff. On the morning of 30 November he went to Hirohito with something the admirals had not said out loud in the conference rooms: the navy hoped to avoid a war with the United States.

The Emperor took it seriously. A little past three that afternoon he sent for Prime Minister Hideki Tojo and told him what his brother had said. Tojo gave his answer after the war, in testimony before the International Military Tribunal in Tokyo.

In their words. Prime Minister Hideki Tojo, recalling his audience with the Emperor on 30 November 1941

"It is the common desire of your Government and Imperial General Headquarters to avoid this war. The Liaison Conference, after prudent and thorough deliberation, arrived at the decision, which has already been reported to the Throne informally, that there was no alternative but to resort to a war of self-defense. If, however, your Majesty still entertains any slight doubt on this matter, it would be wise to summon the Chief of Navy General Staff and the Navy Minister and have them explain the situation more fully."

Testimony of Hideki Tojo before the International Military Tribunal for the Far East, in U.S. Army, Japanese Monograph No. 150, Appendix 4

The Emperor did exactly that. Navy Minister Shigetaro Shimada and Admiral Osami Nagano, Chief of the Naval General Staff, came to the palace that evening. They assured him that the navy was ready and confident. At about seven o'clock, the Lord Keeper of the Privy Seal, Marquis Koichi Kido, telephoned Tojo: the Emperor would allow the Imperial Conference to meet the next day.

It was the last serious doubt to reach the Emperor before his council met. The two admirals who answered it had signed the strike force's orders themselves, and the carriers were already four days out, far beyond the reach of second thoughts.

The Decision

Imperial Palace, Tokyo, 1 December 1941

Day by Day · Day 6 of 13
6:30 a.m. MT, 1 Dec 2026

Tokyo decides on war

Japan's leaders gather before the Emperor today for a meeting whose outcome is already settled.

At the Imperial Conference, Prime Minister Hideki Tojo reports that the talks with Washington have failed. The army and navy say war cannot be avoided. The resolution passes: the Empire will go to war with the United States, Britain and the Netherlands. The Emperor gives his approval.

Far out in the North Pacific, the carriers are already five days out from the Kurils.

Open the day page

The Imperial Conference met that afternoon in Room East 1 of the palace, with the Emperor seated before the cabinet, the chiefs of the army and navy staffs, and Yoshimichi Hara, President of the Privy Council. There was one item on the agenda.

Primary document. The agenda sheet of the Imperial Conference, 1 December 1941

"Commencement of hostilities against the United States, Great Britain and the Netherlands. Negotiations with the United States, based on the 'Principles for the Execution of the National Policy' decided on 5 November, finally fell through. Japan will open war against the United States, Great Britain and the Netherlands."

U.S. Army, Japanese Monograph No. 150, Political Strategy Prior to Outbreak of War

Tojo reported that the talks in Washington had failed and that Japan, "in order to preserve her Empire, must open hostilities." Admiral Nagano, speaking for both general staffs, said the army and navy were ready to act the moment the Emperor gave the order. The finance and agriculture ministers spoke on money and food.

Then Hara asked the questions he was expected to ask on the Emperor's behalf. Did the Americans mean Manchuria when they said China? Would the British battleship Prince of Wales, now on its way east, upset the plans? What would be done for the people of Tokyo if the city burned? At the end he gave his verdict, and it carried a warning inside it.

In their words. Yoshimichi Hara, President of the Privy Council, at the Imperial Conference

"Victory in the initial phase of the war is beyond doubt but carrying out a protracted war under favorable conditions will be extremely difficult, because Japan is not able to stockpile vast quantities of war materials. ... As there is no alternative, I am compelled to give my consent to this plan."

Japanese Monograph No. 150

Tojo promised that the government would work to end the war as early as possible, and that operations could still be stopped if the United States gave way. The resolution passed. The Emperor did not speak; by custom his approval was given by his presence. The plan on the map above, the strike on Hawaii and the conquests in the south, was now the policy of the Japanese Empire.

Climb Mount Niitaka

North Pacific Ocean, 2 December 1941

Day by Day · Day 7 of 13
6:30 a.m. MT, 2 Dec 2026

Climb Mount Niitaka

Three words go out by radio this evening, and the carriers steaming east know exactly what they mean.

"Niitakayama nobore 1208." Climb Mount Niitaka, the highest peak in the Empire. The number is the date: hostilities begin on 8 December, Tokyo time. In Hawaii that is Sunday, 7 December.

The signal reaches every unit of the Imperial Japanese Navy. Aboard the warships, sealed orders are opened. Only a recall can stop them now.

Open the day page

In the evening of 2 December, Tokyo time, the Combined Fleet sent out a short signal: Niitakayama nobore 1208. Climb Mount Niitaka, the highest peak in the Empire. It meant that negotiations had failed and that the war would begin on 8 December, Tokyo time, which in Hawaii would be Sunday, 7 December. The postwar Strategic Bombing Survey put it plainly: on that day the strike force received word that "8 December was designated as 'X'-day."

The orders the carriers had sailed under spelled out what would happen if they were seen on the way. The Survey summarized them from the Japanese records.

From the record. The strike force's instructions for the approach to Hawaii

"If discovered prior to 'X'-minus-2-day, the Task Force was to return to Japan without executing the attack. If discovered prior to 'X'-1-day, the decision as to what action to take was the responsibility of the Task Force Commander. If discovered on 'X'-minus-1-day or the morning of [X]-day the Task Force was to continue with the attack. If at any time during the approach to Pearl Harbor the negotiations with the United States had been successful the attack would have been cancelled."

U.S. Strategic Bombing Survey, The Campaigns of the Pacific War (1946)

The next day the tankers came alongside in the heavy swell and the fleet refueled, the hardest seamanship of the voyage, and it went without a mishap.

A few hours after the signal went out, on the morning of 2 December in Hawaii, Admiral Husband Kimmel was reading a list of where his intelligence officer believed the Japanese fleet to be. Lieutenant Commander Edwin Layton had left two of Japan's carrier divisions off it. Their radio call signs had not been heard for more than two weeks. Layton told the story to Congress in 1946.

In their words. Captain Edwin T. Layton, Pacific Fleet intelligence officer, testifying before the Joint Committee, 1946

"I believe that Admiral Kimmel said, 'What, you do not know where the carriers are?' And my reply was as you read it, or words to that effect. I must say that his saying 'You mean they could be rounding Diamond Head,' was to emphasize the fact that I didn't know where they were."

Joint Committee on the Investigation of the Pearl Harbor Attack, hearings transcript, page 4839

Layton had answered that he hoped they would be sighted before then. Silent carriers usually meant ships in home waters, in port or under refit, and that is how the gap was read. They were far to the north of Midway, refueling for the run south.

Burn the Codes

Washington, 3 December 1941

Day by Day · Day 8 of 13
6:30 a.m. MT, 3 Dec 2026

Japan's embassies burn their codes

Embassies burn their codes when they expect a war. Today Washington warns its admirals that Japan's embassies are doing exactly that.

The Navy tells Admiral Kimmel and the Asiatic Fleet that Tokyo has sent "categoric and urgent instructions" to its posts in Hong Kong, Singapore, Batavia, Manila, Washington and London to destroy most of their codes and ciphers.

Washington can read Japan's secret diplomatic traffic thanks to a copy of the Purple cipher machine that Army codebreakers built without ever seeing the real one.

Open the day page

An embassy burns its codes when it expects to be at war and does not want the enemy to find them. In the first days of December, American codebreakers read Tokyo ordering exactly that. A circular of 1 December told Washington that the posts in London, Hong Kong, Singapore and Manila had been told to destroy their code machines. On 2 December Tokyo told its own embassy in Washington to burn all its codes but one, burn its secret papers, and be ready to destroy a cipher machine.

On 3 December the Navy passed the news to its admirals in the Pacific.

Primary document. Chief of Naval Operations to the Asiatic and Pacific Fleets, 3 December 1941 (dispatch 031850)

"Highly reliable information has been received that categoric and urgent instructions were sent yesterday to Japanese diplomatic and consular posts at Hongkong, Singapore, Batavia, Manila, Washington and London to destroy most of their codes and ciphers at once and to burn all other important confidential and secret documents."

Exhibit No. 11, Hart Inquiry, Pearl Harbor Attack hearings

The message did not say where the blow would fall, and it did not mention the Japanese consulate in Honolulu. In Hawaii it was read as one more sign of a war that was coming somewhere to the west. Few people in Washington or Pearl Harbor knew how the "highly reliable information" had been obtained. Army codebreakers had built copies of Japan's Purple cipher machine without ever seeing one, and could read its diplomatic traffic almost as fast as the embassy could.

That traffic never carried a word about the carriers. The best intelligence operation in the American government was reading every secret but the one that mattered.

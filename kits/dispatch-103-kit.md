## YouTube Shorts description
Watch for the gun director painted with swastikas. Antwerp's gunners were forbidden to fire at anything but the V-1.

Antwerp, liberated on 4 September 1944, was the supply port the Allied armies needed. Once the approaches were cleared of mines, it officially opened to Allied shipping on 28 November 1944. Germany answered with V weapons. The first V-1 flying bomb was recorded on 11 October, and the heavy V-1 bombardment began on 21 October. To fight the flying bombs with guns alone, the Allies created a special and secret command in October 1944, Antwerp X, directed by Brig. Gen. Clare H. Armstrong of the US 50th Antiaircraft Artillery Brigade. To keep their positions secret, the gun crews were strictly forbidden to fire at German aircraft. Their only target was the V-1. The most effective weapon was the US 90mm gun, aimed by the SCR-584 radar, which acquired and tracked an incoming V-1, coupled to the M9 director, which aimed the guns along its path. The proximity fuze, which burst a shell when it came close to its target, had already made this combination deadly against V-1s on the English coast. Antwerp's gunners used it too. A Signal Corps cameraman filmed one battery near "Mansfield, Belgium" on 29 November 1944: a 90mm gun with an M9 director firing, quad .50 caliber machine guns, a radar antenna, and a gun director with many swastikas painted on it. Field Marshal Montgomery asked Armstrong to knock down 50 percent of the bombs that came into the area. A 1945 headquarters release said Armstrong was not satisfied with 50 per cent kills. The command grew to about 22,000 American, British and Polish troops, with 208 US 90mm guns at its height. The guns ran 22 hours a day, and for much of December to March nearly every 90mm barrel produced went to Antwerp. Of 4,883 flying bombs detected, 2,759 were accurately aimed at Antwerp, and only 211 reached the target area. Antwerp X destroyed 2,183. Against bombs aimed at the vital area the kill rate climbed from 52 percent in December to 85 percent in March. In one six day stretch in March the guns destroyed 89 of 91, or 97.8 percent. The last V-1 attack came on 30 March 1945. Antwerp X lost 32 soldiers killed, and its guns fired 532,000 rounds of 90mm ammunition.

This is U.S. Army Signal Corps film of an antiaircraft battery in Belgium on 29 November 1944, titled on its shot card "AA battery knocks out buzz bombs": a 90mm gun crew at the gun, guns in silhouette, the M9 gun director in its pit, a director painted with rows of swastikas, and the radar antenna. The shot card places it near "Mansfield, Belgium", a spelling we could not identify. The original film is silent.

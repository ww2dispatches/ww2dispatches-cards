## YouTube Shorts description
New to Africa, the 2nd Hampshires held their ridge against 10th Panzer. Tunis lay only about twelve miles away.

In late November 1942, less than three weeks after the Torch landings, British and American columns raced east for Tunis. By 28 November British troops held a ridge above Djedeida, on the plain that runs down to the city, some twelve miles away. On the night of 29 November the 2nd Battalion, The Hampshire Regiment, newly arrived in Africa, took over the line in front of Djedeida, east of Tebourba. At 7.45 a.m. on 1 December the Germans struck back. General Wolfgang Fischer's 10th Panzer Division led the counterattack, with parachute infantry of Group Koch pinning the Allies further south. The Hampshires held their ridge. The U.S. Army's official history says their stubborn resistance held the attackers far short of their objective. American tanks of Combat Command B, 1st Armored Division, joined the fight. On 2 December a sortie by light tanks of the 13th Armored Regiment was driven back; outgunned and mired off the road, nineteen were lost. On the afternoon of 3 December the Germans took a high feature on the Hampshires' flank. Counterattacks failed. Major Herbert Wallace Le Patourel, 26, then led four volunteers up the hill under very heavy fire and silenced several machine gun posts. When the rest of his party had all been killed or wounded, Le Patourel went forward alone with a pistol and some grenades to attack the machine guns at close quarters. He did not come back. That night the survivors pulled out under fire along a track by the river, abandoning guns and vehicles and escaping across country in small groups. Tebourba fell at 11 a.m. on 4 December. The Hampshires lost 495 men, about 72 percent of the battalion. Their brigade's three battalions were reduced to about 660 men, plus stragglers. Fischer claimed 55 tanks and more than 1,000 prisoners in four days. On 9 March 1943 the London Gazette announced Le Patourel's Victoria Cross as a posthumous award. Based on reports from wounded men, the citation said he had died of wounds. He had not. A correction in the next week's Gazette deleted the word posthumous: he was a prisoner of war. Badly wounded and captured, he was repatriated in 1943 and received his Cross in Cairo. The Allies had come within about twelve miles of Tunis. They did not get there until 7 May 1943, five months after Tebourba.

This is British and American film of the Allied drive on Tunis in late 1942, from the U.S. and British film Tunisian Victory (1944): shell bursts among vehicles on the plain, a half track gun firing, armor crossing open country under fire. It is not dated to 1 December or filmed at Tebourba itself. The film's narration and score are left out, so the clip runs silent.

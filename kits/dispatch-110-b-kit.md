## YouTube Shorts description
This is the bomber type that wrecked a crowded Allied port in a single evening. Between 17 and 28 ships were sunk or destroyed.

Bari, on Italy's Adriatic coast, was the main supply port for the British Eighth Army in December 1943 and home to the headquarters of the new U.S. Fifteenth Air Force. Few expected a German raid. On the evening of 2 December 1943 about 105 Ju 88 bombers of the Luftwaffe's Luftflotte 2 came in from the east. The docks were brightly lit and the harbor was packed with ships. There was no time to get underway. Between 17 and 28 Allied ships were sunk or destroyed, depending on the source. Five were American Liberty ships. One was the SS John Harvey. In secret she carried 2,000 bombs, each filled with 60 to 70 pounds of sulfur mustard, a reserve to answer in kind if Germany used gas first. Hardly anyone ashore knew. Burning debris set her alight and she blew up, killing everyone aboard. Mustard from the broken bombs mixed with the oil on the water and the billowing smoke. Hospitals swamped with casualties left men in their oil soaked clothes. Hours later came burns, blisters and temporary blindness, and patients began to die in ways the doctors could not explain. Lt. Col. Stewart F. Alexander, 29, a U.S. Army doctor trained in chemical warfare, was sent from Allied headquarters in Algiers. By mapping where each man had been and how he was burned, he traced the injuries to mustard. When the diagnosis reached London, Winston Churchill replied that he did not believe there was mustard in Bari. The gas was kept secret, and deaths were recorded as burns due to enemy action. More than 600 military and merchant marine men were treated for mustard exposure, and 83 of them died. Close to 1,000 civilians in the town were also killed in the raid. Alexander's report recorded something new: in the gassed men, white blood cell counts collapsed, and lymphocytes were the first to disappear. That finding fed the postwar push for cancer drugs. Cornelius Rhoads, former medical chief of the Chemical Warfare Service, saw it as a breakthrough. At Memorial Hospital in New York he pushed the search for mustard drugs that could kill cancer cells, and the Sloan Kettering Institute was announced in 1945. Bari did not start chemotherapy alone. At Yale, a dying lymphoma patient had already received the first dose of nitrogen mustard on 27 August 1942, in wartime secrecy. Alexander's report added proof that the poison targeted the cells cancer doctors wanted to kill.

This is German and British film of the Ju 88, from the British training film Recognizing the Ju 88 (1943), held by the U.S. National Archives as Army training film 111-TF-1-3684. It is not film of Bari; it shows the type of bomber that made the raid. The clip runs without its narration.

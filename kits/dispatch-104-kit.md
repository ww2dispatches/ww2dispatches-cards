## YouTube Shorts description
A fresh armored infantry battalion fought all day for one open height above Grosshau. It took Hill 401.3 and paid with half its strength.

With Grosshau taken, on the night of 29 November 1944 the 4th Division's commander, General Barton, turned Lanham's 22nd Infantry northeast toward Gey and Düren, and attached the 5th Armored Division's 46th Armored Infantry Battalion to it. On 30 November Lanham sent the armored infantry up Hill 401.3, an open height that commanded the whole Grosshau clearing, so they could push on into the woods and guard the regiment's right flank. German fire poured down the open slopes. The battalion fought for the hill all day and took it at nightfall, through what the official history calls sheer determination. In that one day the fresh battalion lost half its strength. Its commander, Lt. Col. William H. Burton Jr., received the Distinguished Service Cross for 30 November 1944, and the official history credits much of the success to him. Staff Sergeants Robert M. Henley and Brady O. Kelley also received the DSC for the same attack. Beside the hill, Major Blazzard's 2nd Battalion of the 22nd fought all day just to reach its starting line at the woods east of Grosshau. Its two attacking companies had fewer than a hundred men between them. On the left it went better. Behind a bank of crossfire from fourteen tanks and tank destroyers moving on both flanks, a battalion of the 22nd was dug in by nightfall at the edge of the woods overlooking Gey. On 1 December Lanham reluctantly committed his last reserve battalion. With Hill 401.3 in American hands and a smoke screen drifting across the open ground, it reached the woods line by nightfall. Sixteen days after the offensive began, the 22nd Infantry, or what was left of it, was through the Hürtgen Forest. Lanham stripped his antitank, headquarters and service companies to form a reserve. Early on 2 December a German company struck from Gey, broke the line and surrounded a battalion command post. Quick artillery fire and that makeshift reserve saved the day. Barton reported that the 22nd had been milked of all offensive ability. All its junior leaders had fallen, so no one was left to show the brave but untrained replacements how to fight. Its relief began on 3 December. Accounts put the 22nd Infantry's losses in its 18 days in the forest at more than 2,700 men, about 85 percent of a regiment of some 3,250. Sears gives 2,773 of 3,257; the regiment's history on the 5th Armored Division site gives 2,774. For the whole 4th Division the official history counts 4,053 battle casualties, including 432 known dead and 255 missing, plus about 2,000 men lost to trench foot, respiratory disease and combat exhaustion, all for an advance of a little over three miles.

This is U.S. Army Signal Corps film shot at Grosshau on 30 November 1944, the day of the attack on Hill 401.3: a tank firing from the edge of the woods, tanks on the muddy road into the ruined village, a gun barrel at the moment of firing, and a crewman with stacked shells. The original film is silent.

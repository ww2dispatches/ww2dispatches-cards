## YouTube Shorts description
Drums, fifes and marching boys. On 1 December 1936 a new law declared all German youth in the Reich organized in the Hitler Youth.

On 1 December 1936 Hitler and Hans Lammers, head of the Reich Chancellery, signed the Law on the Hitler Youth. Its first article said that all German youth within the Reich was organized in the Hitler Youth. The law gave the Hitler Youth the job of raising the young, alongside home and school, physically, intellectually and morally in the spirit of National Socialism. Baldur von Schirach, the Nazi party's youth leader, became Youth Leader of the German Reich. His office became a top Reich authority in Berlin, answering directly to Hitler. The movement had grown fast under pressure and incentives: about 100,000 members in January 1933, more than 5 million by the end of 1936, and 5.4 million, about 65 percent of Germans aged 10 to 18, in 1937. Jewish children were barred. The law itself named no penalty, and historians still argue over how far it made membership compulsory. A decree of March 1939, the Youth Service Ordinance, made service a legal duty for all young Germans aged 10 to 18. The Nuremberg tribunal found that by the time formal conscription came, 97 percent of those eligible were already members. Schirach left the youth post in 1940 to become Gauleiter of Vienna, where he took part in the deportation of the city's remaining Jews. He was replaced by Artur Axmann, 27. Boys raised in the movement went to war. In 1943 the Waffen SS formed a division from Hitler Youth members born in 1926, aged 16 and 17; it fought in Normandy and the Ardennes and took part in massacres in France. In the last months the Volkssturm militia called up males from 16 to 60, many of them Hitler Youth. In March 1945, 16 year old Willi Hübner was decorated with the Iron Cross after the fighting for Lauban in Silesia. Thousands of boys fought and died even as defeat became certain. At Nuremberg on 24 May 1946 Schirach told the court: "The guilt is mine in that I educated the youth of Germany for a man who murdered by the millions." On 1 October 1946 the tribunal acquitted him of crimes against peace but convicted him of crimes against humanity for his part in the Vienna deportations. He served his full 20 years in Spandau, was released on 1 October 1966, and died in 1974.

This is German film of the Hitler Youth from the 1930s, as used in the U.S. War Department film Prelude to War (1942), the first of the Why We Fight series. It is not dated to 1 December 1936, and it was made to show the movement as the regime wanted it seen. The clip runs without its soundtrack.

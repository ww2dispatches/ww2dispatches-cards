# This Week in WW2 Dispatches: Six little ships against the biggest battleship in the world

*Preview line: Our first two weeks, from the torpedoed Kearny to the lost Reuben James, in thirty dispatches.*

Welcome to the first issue of The Weekly Dispatch. WW2 Dispatches launched on Saturday 17 October, and this issue gathers everything from that first morning through Saturday 31 October: fifteen morning dispatches and fifteen evening cards. From next week on, each issue covers a single week.

## Dispatch No. 001: USS Kearny torpedoed

*17 October 1941 · Battle Lines*

Seven weeks before Pearl Harbor, off Iceland, the destroyer USS Kearny raced in to help a British convoy under attack by a German wolf pack, and U-568 put a torpedo into her starboard side. Eleven men were killed and 22 wounded. Her crew sealed off the flooding and kept her moving on the aft engine, and she limped into Iceland two days later.

## Dispatch No. 003: The Nuremberg indictment is filed

*18 October 1945 · Statecraft*

The trial of the century began not in Nuremberg but in Berlin, where the new International Military Tribunal held its first session and four Allied prosecutors lodged the indictment. It named 24 leading Nazis on four counts, including crimes against humanity, a charge new to international law. The trial itself opened in Nuremberg on 20 November 1945.

## Dispatch No. 005: Tin cans go to war

*19 October 1942 · Home Front*

The War Production Board ordered every American town of more than 25,000 people to collect tin cans. Japan's conquest of Southeast Asia had cut off nearly 90 percent of America's tin. Families peeled, rinsed, cut and flattened their cans, and Boy Scouts ran the collections.

## Dispatch No. 007: MacArthur returns to Leyte

*20 October 1944 · Battle Lines*

Two and a half years after promising "I shall return," General Douglas MacArthur waded ashore at Red Beach on Leyte with Philippine President Sergio Osmeña. Army photographer Gaetano Faillace caught one of the most famous pictures of the war. Within days, the largest naval battle in history erupted around the island.

## Dispatch No. 009: Aachen surrenders

*21 October 1944 · Battle Lines*

After five and a half weeks of fighting, the last German garrison in Aachen surrendered, and for the first time a German city fell to the Allies. Hitler had ordered Charlemagne's old capital held at all costs. American troops fought through the ruins house by house, and about 5,600 Germans went into captivity.

## Dispatch No. 011: Mark Clark's secret landing

*22 October 1942 · Statecraft*

Major General Mark Clark paddled ashore in Algeria from the submarine HMS Seraph to win over the French army before the Allied landings. When the police came knocking, he hid in a wine cellar while his hosts faked a party upstairs. Escaping through heavy surf, he lost his trousers and the gold in his money belt. Seventeen days later came Operation Torch.

## Dispatch No. 013: The barrage at El Alamein

*23 October 1942 · Battle Lines*

At 9:40 p.m. nearly 900 British and Commonwealth guns opened fire at once along the line at El Alamein. Behind the barrage, Montgomery's Eighth Army began clearing paths through the minefields the Germans called the Devil's Gardens. Rommel was in Germany on sick leave, and his stand in died of a heart attack the next day.

## Dispatch No. 015: UN Charter takes effect

*24 October 1945 · Statecraft*

The United Nations Charter took effect once the five great powers and a majority of the other signers had ratified it. Fifty nations signed in San Francisco that June, and Poland made 51. The new body was built with a stronger Security Council than the League of Nations that failed in the 1930s.

## Dispatch No. 017: The last stand of Taffy 3

*25 October 1944 · Battle Lines*

Off Samar, six small escort carriers and seven little warships of Taffy 3 saw the giant battleship Yamato and the rest of Kurita's Center Force coming for them. The destroyers and destroyer escorts turned and charged the battleships with torpedoes while planes from the little carriers attacked again and again. At 9:20, Kurita broke off the battle.

## Dispatch No. 019: Hornet's last fight

*26 October 1942 · Battle Lines*

Six months after carrying the Doolittle Raiders, USS Hornet was left dead in the water at the Battle of the Santa Cruz Islands after a Japanese dive bomber struck her stack. American destroyers could not sink the wreck, and Japanese destroyers finished her that night. Japan won the day but lost about 100 aircraft and many of its best crews.

## Dispatch No. 021: The guns at Outpost Snipe

*27 October 1942 · Battle Lines*

At Outpost Snipe, nineteen six pounder antitank guns of the Rifle Brigade and the Royal Artillery held a desert hollow against German and Italian tanks all day. Lieutenant Colonel Victor Turner joined one crew as loader and kept going with a shell fragment in his head. Between 52 and 57 enemy armored vehicles were later counted around Snipe, and Turner received the Victoria Cross.

## Dispatch No. 023: Greece says no

*28 October 1940 · Statecraft*

At three in the morning, Italy's ambassador handed Greek Prime Minister Ioannis Metaxas an ultimatum from Mussolini. Metaxas answered in one sentence: "Alors, c'est la guerre." The outnumbered Greek army stopped the invasion by mid November, and Greece still marks the day as Ochi Day.

## Dispatch No. 025: America's draft lottery

*29 October 1940 · Home Front*

Blindfolded, Secretary of War Henry Stimson reached into a giant glass bowl in Washington, and President Roosevelt read the first number of America's first peacetime draft aloud: 158. Families listened on the radio as every number came out. The men called that winter expected to serve one year.

## Dispatch No. 027: Rescue of the Lost Battalion

*30 October 1944 · Battle Lines*

In the Vosges Mountains, the Japanese American soldiers of the 442nd Regimental Combat Team attacked uphill for days to reach a battalion of Texans cut off by German troops. On 30 October they broke through, and 211 men of the Lost Battalion walked out. By most counts the 442nd took more casualties than the number of men it saved.

## Dispatch No. 029: Reuben James is sunk

*31 October 1941 · Battle Lines*

Five weeks before Pearl Harbor, a torpedo from U-552 meant for a merchant ship struck the destroyer USS Reuben James near Iceland, and her magazine exploded. Only 44 of her crew survived, and not one officer was among them. She was the first U.S. Navy ship sunk by enemy action in the war against Germany.
